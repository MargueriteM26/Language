BOY, 10, PREDICTS
BRITAIN'S WEATHER

Adam steps in front of the green screen at the National Forecasting Centre —
and delivers a flawless forecast for the whole United Kingdom
By Our Science Correspondent  ·  Thursday, 30th July 2026

A ten-year-old schoolboy from London has done what few adults ever get the chance to do: stand in front of a professional green screen at the National Weather Forecasting Centre and deliver a live forecast for the entire United Kingdom.

Adam — whose surname is being withheld — was visiting the centre in Reading as part of a school trip when senior meteorologistMeteorologist — a scientist who studies and predicts the weather Dr Anita Patel invited him to present the day's forecastingForecasting — predicting future events using data and patterns.

"He read every line clearly and confidently," Dr Patel told The Weather Gazette. "He pointed to the right places on the map, named the pressure systems correctly, and didn't hesitate once. Frankly, he was better than some of our interns."

Adam's class had spent the morning exploring the centre's vast control room — a space lined wall-to-wall with screens showing satellite images of the Earth's atmosphereAtmosphere — the layers of air and gas surrounding the Earth from orbit.

It was here that Adam first came face to face with a turbulentTurbulent — moving in an irregular, disturbed, unpredictable way low-pressure system swirling over the North Atlantic — a mass of cloud large enough to cover the entirety of Scotland.

"He was better than some of our interns," said Dr Patel. "He didn't hesitate once."

The highlight of the trip — for Adam at least — was a radar room where precipitationPrecipitation — water falling from clouds: rain, snow, sleet or hail levels across the country are tracked in real time. A vivid yellow and orange blob on the screen indicated heavy rainfall moving slowly towards London.

☁️ What Are Isobars?

IsobarsIsobars — lines on a weather map connecting places with the same air pressure are curved lines drawn on weather maps that connect places sharing the same air pressure. When isobars are packed closely together, expect stormy, turbulent weather. When they are spread apart, conditions are likely to be calm and settled.

Adam described the experience to our reporter as "completely unlike anything I expected." He said that seeing the swirling storm system on the screen — a living, moving mass of cloud and energy — made him think about all the ordinary people going about their lives who had no idea what was coming.

"That's the whole point," Dr Patel explained. "We exist so that people do know what's coming. A warning issued twenty-four hours early can save lives."

The Met Office supercomputer — one of the most powerful in the world — performs trillions of calculations every second to model the entire Earth's atmosphereAtmosphere — the layers of air and gas surrounding the Earth and predict how weather patterns will evolve.

⚡ Fast Facts

  • The UK has over 200 automated weather stations
  • Weather balloons are released twice daily worldwide
  • Modern 5-day forecasts are as accurate as 1-day forecasts were in the 1980s
  • The Met Office computer does 16,000 trillion calculations per second

For Adam, the visit ended with what he called — choosing his words with care — "a bit of a phenomenonPhenomenon — a remarkable or extraordinary event or thing that can be observed." His classmate Priya, who watched from the control room, was rather more direct: "He was absolutely brilliant. I was so jealous."

Adam's school has since been invited back for a full day's work experience at the centre next term. Dr Patel says she hopes he will consider a career in meteorology. Adam, however, says he is still deciding between weather forecasting and driving trains.
