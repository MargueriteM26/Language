Adam and the Storm Chasers

approx. 450 words · Year 5

The minibus pulled up outside a low, grey building on the edge of Reading, and Adam pressed his nose against the rain-streaked window. A sign above the entrance read: National Weather Forecasting Centre. This was no ordinary school trip.

Inside, a meteorologistMeteorologist — a scientist who studies and predicts the weather named Dr Patel was waiting for them. She wore a lanyard covered in tiny cloud badges and spoke at the speed of a storm.

"Welcome, everyone. Today you'll see exactly how we predict the weather — from the first raindrop falling somewhere over the Atlantic, all the way to the forecast on your phone tomorrow morning."

Adam followed the group into a vast control room. Enormous screens covered every wall, each one showing swirling patterns of white and grey — satellite images of the atmosphereAtmosphere — the layers of air and gas surrounding the Earth from space. The room hummed with the sound of computers and quiet, urgent voices.

"Those white spirals," whispered Adam's friend Priya, pointing at the largest screen, "they look like they're alive."

"That's because they nearly are," said Dr Patel, who had appeared silently beside them. "That's a turbulentTurbulent — moving in an irregular, disturbed way; rough and unpredictable low-pressure system heading towards Scotland. We're forecastingForecasting — predicting future events using data and patterns winds of up to seventy miles per hour by Thursday."

Adam stared at the screen. The swirling mass of cloud looked enormous. He thought about the people in Scotland going about their Tuesday, with no idea what was spinning towards them across the sea.

Later, Dr Patel showed them a room where precipitationPrecipitation — water falling from clouds in any form: rain, snow, sleet or hail levels were measured using radar. On a map covered in isobarsIsobars — lines on a weather map that connect places with the same air pressure — curved lines linking areas of equal air pressure — a deep yellow and orange blob sat like a bruise over the west of England.

"Rain," said Dr Patel simply. "Lots of it. By tomorrow, that yellow blob will be sitting right over London."

Then came the moment Adam hadn't expected. Dr Patel turned to him.

"Would you like to present the forecast?"

Adam's mouth went dry. But he walked to the green screen, took the script Dr Patel handed him, and looked straight into the camera. He read each line clearly, pointing to the map as he went.

When he finished, the control room gave a small round of applause. Priya grinned at him. Adam grinned back. He had just predicted — with some help — tomorrow's weather for the whole of the United Kingdom. That, he decided, was a phenomenonPhenomenon — a remarkable or extraordinary event or thing that can be observed.

Weather Forecasting

How Do We Know What Tomorrow's Weather Will Be?

approx. 370 words · Informational · Year 5

Have you ever wondered how the weather presenter on television knows it will rain on Thursday — three days before a single cloud appears in the sky? The answer lies in an extraordinary mix of science, technology, and mathematics.

Watching from Space

The first clue comes from satellites orbiting the Earth far above the atmosphereAtmosphere — the layers of air and gas surrounding the Earth. These spacecraft send back thousands of images every hour, allowing meteorologistsMeteorologist — a scientist who studies and predicts the weather — scientists who study the weather — to watch weather systems developing across entire oceans.

Measuring from the Ground

At the same time, thousands of weather stations dotted across the globe measure air temperature, wind speed, and precipitationPrecipitation — water falling from clouds: rain, snow, sleet or hail — the collective name for all forms of water falling from clouds, including rain, snow, sleet, and hail. Radar systems detect where precipitation is falling right now and how heavy it is. Balloons carrying sensors are released twice a day from stations all over the world to measure the upper atmosphereAtmosphere — the layers of air and gas surrounding the Earth.

☁️ Did you know? The UK alone has over 200 automated weather stations collecting data every single minute of the day and night.

Reading the Maps

All this data is plotted onto weather maps using isobarsIsobars — lines on a weather map connecting places of equal air pressure — curved lines that connect places sharing the same air pressure. Where isobars are packed closely together, the air pressure is changing rapidly, meaning turbulentTurbulent — moving in an irregular, disturbed way; rough and unpredictable, stormy weather is likely. Where they are spread apart, conditions are calm.

Supercomputers Do the Maths

No human brain could process all this data alone. The forecastingForecasting — predicting future events using data and patterns is done by some of the most powerful supercomputers on the planet. The Met Office computer in Exeter performs around 16,000 trillion calculations every second, running a model of the Earth's entire atmosphere to predict how weather patterns will move and change.

Why It Matters

Accurate weather forecastingForecasting — predicting future events using data and patterns saves lives. It helps farmers decide when to plant crops, allows airlines to plan safe routes, and gives emergency services time to prepare for dangerous conditions. A severe storm is a powerful natural phenomenonPhenomenon — a remarkable or extraordinary event or thing that can be observed — but with modern science, we can see it coming days in advance.

🌍 Did you know? A modern five-day weather forecast is as accurate today as a one-day forecast was in the 1980s. Technology has made us much better at predicting the weather!
